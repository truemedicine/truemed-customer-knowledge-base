HSA/FSA reimbursement claims can be denied for several reasons, from missing documentation to product eligibility questions or administrator-specific requirements.

This guide explains the most common denial reasons, what each one means, and how to fix it. Many denials can be resolved by correcting your documentation, resubmitting your claim, or filing an appeal.

## **Who decides whether my claim is approved?**

Your HSA/FSA administrator reviews your reimbursement claim and makes the initial decision to approve or deny it. Truemed facilitates the qualification process and coordinates delivery of the Letter of Medical Necessity (LMN) from the clinician to you. 

If your claim is denied, Truemed can work with you to address the denial by providing additional documentation, helping update your LMN when appropriate, or providing an appeal letter. While these steps may help resolve a denial, your administrator makes the final decision on your claim.

## **Does an LMN guarantee reimbursement?**

No. An LMN documents why a purchase is medically necessary for your specific health condition, but it doesn't guarantee reimbursement. Each HSA/FSA administrator applies its own review process and documentation requirements.

Some administrators have additional requirements for signature format, provider type, or expense categories. That's why a claim can be denied even when you have an LMN. If your claim is denied, review the reason carefully to determine whether you need corrected documentation or should appeal the decision.

### 1. Doctor or wet signature required

Some administrators deny LMN-based claims on the grounds that the signature must be handwritten rather than electronic. In most cases, this requirement is not supported by U.S. law.

Under the U.S. Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act (15 U.S. Code § 7001), a signature, contract, or record cannot be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because it is in electronic form. An LMN does not fall under any of the narrow exceptions to this rule (such as wills).

**What to do:**

- **Push back with your administrator.** Share the information above and ask them to accept the electronic signature as it's consistent with federal law. If they refuse, ask them to cite the specific source of their handwritten-signature requirement.
- **Contact Truemed if you still need help.** If your administrator continues to require a handwritten signature, email [support@truemed.com](mailto:support@truemed.com). Truemed can often provide an updated LMN in the format your administrator requires.

### 2. Provider address in a different state

Certain administrators flag claims when the address of the provider who signed the LMN is in a different state than the patient's address. This doesn't automatically disqualify your claim, but it can trigger a manual review or outright denial with some plans.

**What to do:** Contact Truemed. Depending on your administrator's policy, there may be steps we can take to address this, or we can help you understand what documentation to provide when you appeal.

### 3. LMNs issued by Nurse Practitioner vs. Medical Doctor

Some administrators only accept Letters of Medical Necessity signed by a Medical Doctor (MD) and will deny claims backed by an LMN from a Nurse Practitioner (NP) or other licensed provider.

Many states have adopted full practice authority allowing Nurse Practitioners to sign letters of medical necessity. However, some states, including Alabama, Mississippi, South Carolina, and Tennessee, often require a physician (MD/DO) to sign an LMN. This is already considered within your Truemed flow, and those states will always include an MD signature.

**What to do:** If your denial cites the provider's credentials, reach out to Truemed. We can sometimes accommodate a request for an LMN signed by an MD to meet your administrator's requirements.

### 4. Preventive LMN is not tied to a specific health condition

Some administrators deny a claim because the LMN lists prevention as the reason for the purchase. Prevention is a recognized purpose under federal tax law. Internal Revenue Code Section 213(d)(1)(A) defines eligible medical expenses as amounts paid “for the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease.” [IRS Publication 502](https://www.google.com/url?q=https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) also confirms that expenses primarily intended to prevent a disease can qualify when they are medically necessary and properly documented.

A preventive LMN still has to be tied to a specific health condition: it documents the condition the purchase is meant to prevent or keep from progressing. Purchases made for general health are not eligible.

**What to do:**

- Resubmit your claim with a short note explaining that your LMN aligns with the tax code and IRS guidance, which explicitly include the prevention of disease. Cite IRC Section 213(d) and IRS Publication 502.
- If your administrator's portal allows it, try a different expense category, such as “Other Medical Expenses,” in case the original category applies a narrower rule.
- If your claim is denied again, email [support@truemed.com](mailto:support@truemed.com) with your denial notice so we can help you with next steps.

### 5. Diagnosis is not linked to your purchase

This denial means the diagnosis listed on your LMN doesn't clearly connect to the product you purchased. For example, a diagnosis focused on one health area may not be sufficient justification for a product in a different category.

**What to do:** In some cases, taking the Truemed qualification survey again can result in a more appropriate diagnosis being linked to your purchase. Contact Truemed to discuss whether a survey retake or an updated LMN would address this denial.

### 6. Product denied as an ineligible expense

Some administrators deny a claim because they consider the product a personal-use item rather than a qualified medical expense. This can happen even when you have an LMN. If your purchase is supported by an LMN, you can appeal using the points below.

IRS Publication 502 describes the types of medical and dental expenses you can deduct on your taxes, which generally also qualify for HSA/FSA reimbursement. It does not list every eligible expense. A product that isn't named in Publication 502 can still qualify when it is used for a medically necessary reason.

A health purchase used for a medically necessary reason can qualify when:

- A Letter of Medical Necessity (LMN) from a healthcare provider shows the expense is needed to treat or prevent a specific health condition.
- The expense is primarily for the prevention or treatment of that condition. The LMN should show a clear clinical connection between the condition and the product. For prevention, it should explain how the product helps prevent the condition from developing or progressing.
- The condition has been diagnosed by a healthcare provider qualified to make that diagnosis.

Truemed LMNs are issued by independent, licensed clinicians in your state and are written to document each of these points.

**What to do:**

- File a formal appeal using the process described in your plan documents. Include the points above and explain how the product treats or prevents your diagnosed condition.
- Include your LMN and itemized receipt with the appeal. For partially eligible products, also include your LMN addendum showing the eligible amount.
- Email [support@truemed.com](mailto:support@truemed.com) with your denial notice if you need help with your appeal.

### 7. Incomplete receipt

Your administrator may deny a claim if the receipt doesn't include enough detail to verify the expense. Administrators typically need an itemized receipt showing the customer's name, purchase date, merchant name, specific product or service, and amount paid. A card statement or order confirmation without individual item details may not be sufficient.

**What to do:&#x20;**&#x52;equest an itemized receipt from the merchant showing the customer's name, specific product or service, purchase date, merchant name, and amount paid. If the merchant can't provide one, contact Truemed. Our team can often provide or create a receipt with the required details. Submit the receipt with your LMN and any other documentation requested by your administrator.

### 8. LMN dated after purchase

For purchases that require an LMN, the documentation generally needs to be issued on or before the purchase date. If your LMN was issued afterward, your administrator may question whether medical necessity was documented at the time of purchase.

**What to do:** Review the[ *retroactive claims guidance*](https://help.truemed.com/reimbursements-claims/retroactive-purchases) for the applicable timing rules and next steps.

### 9. Wrong expense category

Some administrator portals apply narrower rules to certain expense categories. Even when a purchase has supporting medical necessity documentation, the category selected during submission may cause the claim to be flagged or denied.

**What to do:** If your administrator's portal allows it, try resubmitting under a broader category, such as “Other Medical Expenses.” Include your LMN and itemized receipt, and confirm with your administrator which category is appropriate if you're unsure.

## **Can a denied claim be corrected?**

Yes. Many denied claims can be corrected by resubmitting with updated documentation or filing an appeal. Your next step depends on the reason for the denial.

1. **Identify the denial reason.** Read your denial notice carefully. Determine whether the issue relates to documentation, product eligibility, or an administrator-specific policy.
2. **Correct documentation issues.** Contact Truemed first. In many cases, we can update or correct your LMN or other documentation before you resubmit.
3. **Address product eligibility questions.** Contact Truemed to discuss whether your documentation can be strengthened. If the product genuinely doesn't meet eligibility requirements, Truemed will explain your options.
4. **Appeal administrator-specific denials.** Contact Truemed for guidance specific to your administrator, then file a formal appeal with your administrator within the stated deadline.
5. **Escalate if needed.** If your appeal is denied, ask your administrator whether a second-level appeal or independent review is available. For FSA plans, up to three levels of appeal are typically permitted.

For any of these situations, you can reach Truemed at [support@truemed.com](mailto:support@truemed.com). Include your denial notice, the denial reason or code, and your administrator's name so we can give you the most relevant guidance.

## **What documentation should I provide when resubmitting or appealing?**

Gather the documents related to your original purchase and the denial before resubmitting or appealing. Depending on the reason for the denial, include:

- **Denial notice and reason code:** Include the explanation your administrator provided, including any specific documentation requirements.
- **Letter of Medical Necessity (LMN):** Include the LMN that supports the medical necessity of your purchase.
- **Itemized receipt:** Provide a receipt showing the purchase date, merchant, product or service, and amount paid.
- **LMN addendum for partially eligible products:** Include the addendum showing the portion of the purchase that qualifies.
- **Administrator name:** Identify your HSA/FSA administrator and include any relevant claim reference numbers.

Keep copies of all claim documents, receipts, LMNs, addenda, and correspondence with your administrator. HSA reimbursements may be subject to IRS review or audit, so retaining records helps you substantiate that the expense qualified.

For more information about submitting claims and keeping the required documentation, see[ *How to Reimburse*](https://help.truemed.com/reimbursements-claims/how-to-reimburse).

If you need help with a denial, email [support@truemed.com](mailto:support@truemed.com) with your denial notice, the denial reason or code, and your administrator's name. Refer to the documentation checklist above so you can include the relevant records.
